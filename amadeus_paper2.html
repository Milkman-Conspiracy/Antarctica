The White Tiger — WT

Author / YearAravind Adiga, 2008. Booker Prize winner.
FormEpistolary novel — seven letters to Chinese Premier Wen Jiabao.
NarratorBalram Halwai — unreliable, self-promotional, retrospective.
ArcBorn in the "Darkness" (rural Bihar), Balram becomes a driver for a wealthy family (Ashok & Mongoose), murders Ashok, and becomes an entrepreneur in Bangalore.
Key motifsRooster coop, Darkness vs. Light, the White Tiger, chandelier, Big Bellies vs. Small Bellies.
ContextPost-liberalisation India (1990s–2000s). "India Shining" economic miracle masks entrenched caste and class inequality.

Fun Home — FH

Author / YearAlison Bechdel, 2006. Graphic memoir.
FormNon-linear graphic memoir — caption-image interplay; retrospective narration.
NarratorAdult Alison reconstructing her childhood in Beech Creek, Pennsylvania.
ArcGrowing up with a closeted, emotionally abusive gay father (Bruce); Alison's own coming-out at college; Bruce's probable suicide (hit by Sunbeam truck, aged 44).
Key motifsDaedalus/Icarus myth, the house, mirrors, literary allusions (Fitzgerald, Wilde, Colette, Proust), the "fun home" (funeral parlour).
Context1970s–80s rural Pennsylvania. LGBTQ+ identity pre-visibility; conservative American social landscape.
Key comparison axis
Both texts centre on a protagonist who constructs their identity against — or in opposition to — a society that denies their full humanity. In WT this denial is structural and economic; in FH it is cultural and familial. Both use form self-consciously: the epistolary frame in WT and the graphic memoir in FH mean the narrative is always a performance, always shaped by what the narrator chooses to reveal and conceal.
2026 Assessment Changes — Note
From 2026, Criterion B (comparison) is now worth 10/25 marks — double the weight of each other criterion. Every paragraph must explicitly compare, not just describe each text in turn.
Criteria A (understanding), C (structure), D (language) are each 5/25.
Aim: 3 body paragraphs, each structured as PEEL — Point, Evidence, Explain, Link to question. Open each paragraph with a comparative topic sentence.
Identity & Self-Definition
★★★ Most probable
Past paper prompts — this theme
"Examine the dilemmas of identity experienced by the main characters." (Nov 2024)
"Writers often use a character alienated from their culture to explore social values." (Specimen 2013)
"Explore the theme of difference and its consequences in wealth, class or status." (Nov 2024)
"In what ways and for what reasons do works seek to represent thought or interior consciousness?"
The White Tiger
Balram's identity is entirely self-constructed and violently maintained. Born with the caste name "Halwai" (sweetmaker), he re-names himself "White Tiger" — born once in a generation. This re-naming is Adiga's central argument: identity in India is assigned at birth, but Balram forcibly rewrites it. However, Adiga ironises this — as an employer, Balram reproduces the same master/servant hierarchy, suggesting the self can never fully escape its origins. The rooster coop metaphor extends this: even those who break free carry the coop's psychology inside them. His unreliable narration is also an identity performance — he is simultaneously presenting himself as hero, entrepreneur, and murderer.
Unreliable narrator Re-naming / self-naming Irony Epistolary self-presentation
Key Quotes
"Neither you nor I speak English, but there are some things that can be said only in English."
→ Language as class marker; identity tied to access to power structures
"I was looking for the key for years, but the door was always open."
→ Self-created agency; irony that freedom was always available but psychologically blocked
"You ask 'Are you a man or a demon?' Neither, I say. I have woken up, and the rest of you are sleeping."
→ Balram's rejection of conventional moral categories; identity beyond good/evil
"A White Tiger keeps no friends. It's too dangerous."
→ Exceptionalism as isolation; the cost of self-construction
Fun Home
Bechdel constructs identity retrospectively — the memoir is itself the act of self-definition. Alison's lesbian identity emerges gradually, triangulated against her father's repressed queerness. She repeatedly reads herself through literary characters (Colette, Fitzgerald, Wilde), suggesting identity is not discovered but narrated. The gap between the drawn child-self and the writing adult-narrator creates a permanent split: who she was versus who she has made herself. Bruce's identity is the negative image — assembled from surfaces (the house's décor, his students, his affairs) rather than any core. The text argues that suppressed identity kills; expressed identity, however painfully, liberates.
Retrospective narration Intertextuality Caption-image split Daedalus/Icarus myth
Key Quotes
"I had recently discovered some of Dad's old clothes. Putting on a formal shirt with its studs and cufflinks was a nearly mystical pleasure, like finding myself fluent in a language I'd never been taught."
→ Gender identity as inherited yet self-discovered; language metaphor echoes WT
"My homosexuality remained at that point purely theoretical, an untested hypothesis. But it was a hypothesis so thorough and so convincing I saw no reason not to share it immediately."
→ Identity as intellectual construction before experience; Bechdel's analytical self
"I employ these allusions not only as descriptive devices, but because my parents are most real to me in fictional terms."
→ Meta-commentary on the memoir's method; identity mediated through literature
"I'd been upstaged, demoted from protagonist in my own drama to comic relief in my parents' tragedy."
→ Identity as narrative role; self-awareness about being a secondary character in her own story
Sample comparative thesis
While both Adiga and Bechdel present identity as something that must be actively seized rather than passively received, they diverge in the violence of that seizure: where Adiga's Balram literally murders to escape his assigned self, Bechdel's Alison reconstructs hers through the slow, literary labour of memoir — suggesting that the cost of self-definition is always paid in proportion to the force of the society that originally denied it.
Power, Class & Inequality
★★★ Most probable
Past paper prompts — this theme
"Explore the theme of difference and its consequences in wealth, class or status." (Nov 2024)
"What questions about power has your study of two works raised?"
"Analyze how justice is represented and understood." (Specimen 2013)
"Authors use characters who are trapped as a means to critique society."
"Discuss the significance of the rural and/or urban." (May 2015)
The White Tiger
Power in WT is Adiga's central obsession. The novel anatomises post-liberalisation India as a society of two castes: "Men with Big Bellies and Men with Small Bellies." The rooster coop metaphor argues that structural subjugation is most effective when internalised — servants watch their masters commit crimes and do nothing. Power is exercised spatially (Darkness vs. Light), economically (the family as financial hostage), and psychologically (Balram's conditioning). Crucially, Balram's acquisition of power through murder does not dismantle the system — he becomes an employer who reproduces it, revealing capitalism's capacity to absorb transgression without structural change.
Rooster coop metaphor Satirical address Spatial symbolism Dark comedy
Key Quotes
"In the old days there were one thousand castes and destinies in India. These days, there are just two castes: Men with Big Bellies, and Men with Small Bellies. And only two destinies: eat — or get eaten up."
→ Modernisation as false progress; class replaces caste but preserves hierarchy
"Never before in human history have so few owed so much to so many... a servitude so strong that you can put the key of his emancipation in a man's hands and he will throw it back at you with a curse."
→ Internalised oppression; psychological dimensions of class power
"The trustworthiness of servants is the basis of the entire Indian economy."
→ Structural dependency of the elite on the subjugated; sardonic exposure of the system
"The story of a poor man's life is written on his body, in a sharp pen."
→ Class as physical inscription; the body as record of economic violence
Fun Home
Power in FH is domestic and patriarchal. Bruce's power derives not from economic class (he is a schoolteacher and funeral director in a small town) but from his position as patriarch and his control of the family home as an aesthetic project. He treats his children as props in his performance of ideal bourgeois life ("a still life with children"). The house becomes a theatre of control — every object arranged, every renovation a reassertion of dominance. Notably, Bruce's power is also a form of imprisonment: the very control he exerts over others is the mechanism of his own self-suppression. Bechdel's memoir is itself an act of reclaiming power — she refuses to let Bruce's version of the family be the only one.
Architecture as character Theatrical metaphor Retrospective re-framing Patriarchal critique
Key Quotes
"Sometimes, when things were going well, I think my father actually enjoyed having a family. Or at least, the air of authenticity we lent to his exhibit. Sort of like a still life with children."
→ Family as prop; children instrumentalised for Bruce's self-presentation
"I grew to resent the way my father treated his furniture like children, and his children like furniture."
→ Inversion of care; domestic power as dehumanising force
"My father could spin garbage into gold. He was an alchemist of appearance, a savant of surface, a Daedalus of decor."
→ Power through artifice; the myth allusion positions Bruce as a figure of both creation and destruction
"My father used his skillful artifice not to make things, but to make things appear to be what they were not. That is to say, impeccable."
→ Surface as power; the performance of respectability as a form of domination
Sample comparative thesis
Adiga and Bechdel locate power not in grand institutions but in the daily fabric of life — the economic machinery that traps Balram in WT and the domestic theatre that conscripts Alison in FH — arguing collectively that the most durable forms of oppression are those that render themselves invisible by being naturalised as the order of things.
Freedom, Agency & Determinism
★★★ Most probable
Past paper prompts — this theme
"Discuss in what ways and to what effect the 'freedom to act, to speak, to think' has been explored." (Nov 2019)
"Authors use characters who are trapped as a means to critique society."
"Some would say that we can never escape the past."
The White Tiger
Freedom in WT is available only through transgression. Adiga's rooster coop metaphor argues that structural determinism is most powerful when it operates at the psychological level — servants have the physical capacity to rebel but lack the psychological freedom. Balram's murder of Ashok is framed as the only authentic act of free will accessible to someone of his class. Yet Adiga withholds moral endorsement: Balram's freedom is purchased by abandoning his family to likely reprisal and by reproducing the employer/servant hierarchy himself. Freedom without structural change is merely a change of who sits inside the cage.
Rooster coop Dark comedy Ironic Bildungsroman Unreliable narrator
Key Quotes
"Go to Old Delhi and look at the way they keep chickens there in the market... They see the organs of their brothers lying around them. They know they are next, yet they cannot rebel. They do not try to get out of the coop. The very same thing is done with humans in this country."
→ The rooster coop: freedom as psychologically rather than physically blocked
"The moment you recognize what is beautiful in this world, you stop being a slave."
→ Consciousness as the precondition of freedom; aesthetic awakening as political act
"The story of my upbringing is the story of how a half-baked fellow is produced."
→ Incomplete education as the mechanism of unfreedom; denied knowledge as structural oppression
Fun Home
Freedom in FH is inseparable from honesty. Bruce, denied the freedom to live as a gay man in 1970s conservative Pennsylvania, channels his frustrated energy into controlling others — his family, his house, his students. His suicide is Bechdel's "final artifice": a death that preserves the secret rather than dismantling it, the ultimate act of a man who could not be free. Alison's freedom is generational and achieved through speech — her coming-out, her mother's divorce, the memoir itself. The non-linear structure formally enacts this: freedom is not a single rupture but a gradual, retrospective rewriting of what one's life has been and could be.
Non-linear chronology Silence vs. speech Generational contrast Memoir as liberation
Key Quotes
"I suppose that a lifetime spent hiding one's erotic truth could have a cumulative renunciatory effect. Sexual shame is in itself a kind of death."
→ Unfreedom as existential annihilation; the cost of closeted identity
"If it weren't for the unconventionality of my desires, my mind might never have been forced to reckon with my body."
→ Queer identity as the catalyst for self-knowledge; difference as liberating
"My father's death was a queer business — queer in every sense of that multivalent word."
→ Wordplay on 'queer'; death and sexuality intertwined as the ultimate consequence of unfreedom
Sample comparative thesis
Both Adiga and Bechdel present freedom not as an abstract right but as a historically and structurally conditioned possibility: where Adiga grounds its denial in class and economic determinism, Bechdel grounds it in cultural homophobia — both suggesting that the truly unfree are not those who lack opportunity, but those whose inner lives have been colonised by the expectations of others.
Family, Relationships & Belonging
★★★ Most probable
Past paper prompts — this theme
"Discuss how two works connect the past with the present." (Nov 2024)
"How is home depicted and what is its significance?"
"Compare the presentation and significance of older people in at least two works."
"Discuss the role of education and/or learning in at least two works."
The White Tiger
Family in WT is a mechanism of social control. Balram's extended family (grandmother Kusum, siblings) is held as collateral by his employers — an ancient feudal arrangement presented as unremarkable. This "hostage family" motif literalises the argument that class entrapment is collective: you cannot escape because your escape would destroy your kin. Balram's decision to murder Ashok knowing his family will likely be killed is both the novel's moral crisis and its central political argument: individual liberation under capitalism requires the abandonment of communal ties. The family is not a refuge but a leash.
Hostage family motif Collective vs. individual Moral ambiguity
Key Quotes
"See, the poor dream all their lives of getting enough to eat and looking like the rich. And what do the rich dream of? Losing weight and looking like the poor."
→ Class inversion; the desire each class has to be the other reveals the system's absurdity
"If only a man could spit his past out so easily."
→ The impossibility of severing family/origin; the past as adhesive
Fun Home
The father-daughter relationship is Fun Home's structural spine. Bechdel reads her entire childhood through the retroactive lens of her father's secret — a reinterpretation that is both clarifying and destabilising. The family home is the book's central symbol: obsessively renovated by Bruce, it is simultaneously beautiful and false, a stage set for a life he could not actually live. The children are cast as supporting players. Helen is present but peripheral, a figure of silent endurance. The memoir's non-linearity formally enacts the psychological experience of family: we do not experience family sequentially, but through repeated returns to key scenes, reinterpreted as we grow.
House as symbol Myth (Daedalus/Icarus) Non-linear time Theatrical family
Key Quotes
"In our particular reenactment of this relationship, it was not me, but my father who was to plummet from the sky."
→ Inversion of the Icarus myth; the parent's fall, not the child's. Foreshadows Bruce's death.
"It was not a triumphal return. Home, as I had known it, was gone."
→ Home as constructed fiction; its destruction coincides with the family's exposure
"His absence resonated retroactively, echoing back through all the time I knew him... But I ached as if he were already gone."
→ Emotional absence as the defining quality of the father-daughter relationship
"Although I'm good at enumerating my father's flaws, it's hard for me to sustain much anger at him... the bar is lower for fathers than for mothers."
→ Feminist critique embedded in grief; gender asymmetry in parental expectations
Sample comparative thesis
Adiga and Bechdel each present the family as the site where social violence is most intimately felt and most difficult to resist: in WT, the family functions as a literal instrument of class coercion, while in FH it operates as a theatre of enforced normality — in both cases, leaving it requires not merely physical departure but a fundamental psychological rupture.
Gender, Sexuality & the Body
★★★ Most probable
Past paper prompts — this theme
"Compare how writers have explored the themes of love and friendship, or disguise and deceit."
"Examine how works explore the dilemmas of identity experienced by main characters." (Nov 2024)
"Discuss the ways in which at least two writers have sought to undermine or interfere with the 'voices' of their characters."
The White Tiger
Gender in WT is largely a marker of structural absence. Women — grandmother Kusum, Pinky Madam — exist at the margins of Balram's narration, which is itself a political statement about a patriarchal society. Pinky Madam's departure after the hit-and-run is read by Balram as further evidence of elite selfishness, not through a gendered lens. Adiga's marginalisation of women in Balram's narration mirrors their marginalisation in the world he describes. The novel's preoccupation is with the masculine performance of class identity — what it means to "be a man" in a society that denies full manhood to the poor.
Absence as critique Masculine performance Class over gender
Key Quotes
"Let animals live like animals; let humans live like humans. That's my whole philosophy in a sentence."
→ Humanity as denied to the poor; the body as site of class dehumanisation
"The dreams of the rich, and the dreams of the poor — they never overlap, do they?"
→ Class as the primary lens, not gender; the body's desires shaped by economic position
Fun Home
Sexuality is Fun Home's structural and thematic centre. The entire memoir is built around two parallel coming-out stories — Bruce's repressed and fatal, Alison's expressed and liberating. Bechdel places these trajectories against each other as mirror images: same desire, opposite outcomes, entirely determined by historical context and personal courage. Her use of literary allusions (Wilde, Proust, Colette) normalises queerness through canonical precedent. The graphic form is essential here: the drawn body is always already a construction — Bechdel can literalise the performance of gender by depicting Bruce in women's clothes, Alison in men's, without linguistic mediation.
Parallel coming-out narratives Literary allusion Visual gender performance Queer identity as central
Key Quotes
"I suppose that a lifetime spent hiding one's erotic truth could have a cumulative renunciatory effect. Sexual shame is in itself a kind of death."
→ Repressed sexuality as existential self-annihilation; Bruce's fate as consequence
"It's imprecise and insufficient, defining the homosexual as a person whose gender expression is at odds with his or her sex."
→ Bechdel challenges reductive definitions of queer identity; analytical approach to selfhood
"If there was ever a bigger pansy than my father, it was Marcel Proust."
→ Literary allusion to establish queer tradition; dark humour as coping mechanism
"Feminism is the theory. Lesbianism is the practice."
→ Political grounding of sexual identity; queer sexuality as feminist act
Sample comparative thesis
Where Adiga deploys gender as a largely structural absence — its marginalisation of women enacting the marginalisation it describes — Bechdel places sexuality at the absolute centre of her work, arguing that the suppression of queer identity is not a private tragedy but a socially enforced one: in both cases, the body is the terrain on which society inscribes its hierarchies.
Form, Structure & Narrative Technique
★★ Moderately probable
Past paper prompts — this theme
"Writers use structural elements in unusual ways to shape meaning."
"A story has to be told by somebody — compare your impressions of the 'story-tellers'."
"Discuss the ways in which at least two writers have sought to undermine or interfere with the voices of their characters."
"Referring to two works, consider the writers' use of language and the effects this achieves." (May 2025)
"In what ways do at least two works make use of the relationship between content and form?"
The White Tiger
The epistolary form is WT's most significant structural choice. Seven letters over seven nights creates urgency and frames the entire narrative as retrospective self-justification. Balram's address to a foreign premier (Wen Jiabao) is a satirical device: India's contradictions are exposed through the irony of a national self-critique delivered to a rival power. The single first-person voice creates claustrophobia and unreliability simultaneously. Adiga inverts the Bildungsroman: the "rise from poverty" arc is corrupted by murder at its centre, refusing the genre's conventional moral optimism. The epistolary structure also means everything is performative — Balram is always presenting, never confessing.
Epistolary form Unreliable narrator Inverted Bildungsroman Satirical address Dark comedy / bathos
Key Quotes
"It is an ancient and venerated custom of people in my country to start a story by praying to a Higher Power. I guess, Your Excellency, that I too should start off by kissing some god's arse. Which god's arse, though? There are so many choices."
→ Bathos; undermines religious convention; establishes irreverent, unreliable narrative voice immediately
"Strange thoughts brew in your heart when you spend too much time with old books."
→ Self-awareness about literacy as a disruptive force; education as both liberation and danger
Fun Home
The graphic memoir form is Bechdel's most radical formal choice. The interplay between drawn panel and written caption creates a permanent split between showing and telling — the two channels carry different truths simultaneously. Bechdel often describes one thing in captions while the image depicts something slightly different, enacting the instability of memory and the impossibility of objective self-narration. The non-linear chronology follows psychological rather than temporal logic. Intertextuality (Fitzgerald, Wilde, Proust, Colette, Joyce) is not decoration but structural: Bechdel uses literature as a scaffold for understanding, arguing that her family is "most real to her in fictional terms."
Graphic memoir form Caption-image disjunction Non-linear chronology Intertextuality Essayistic prose style
Key Quotes
"I employ these allusions not only as descriptive devices, but because my parents are most real to me in fictional terms. And perhaps my cool aesthetic distance itself does more to convey the arctic climate of our family than any particular literary comparison."
→ Meta-commentary on the memoir's method; distance as revelation; form enacting content
"It's a curiously ineffectual attempt at censorship... In an act of prestidigitation typical of the way my father juggled his public appearance and private reality, the evidence is simultaneously hidden and revealed."
→ Self-aware commentary on concealment; the photograph as structural analogy for the whole memoir
Sample comparative thesis
Both Adiga and Bechdel choose forms that are self-consciously unreliable — the confession that cannot be trusted in WT, the memoir that admits it cannot fully remember in FH — suggesting that literary form itself can enact the central argument of each work: that the self is always a construction, never a transparent fact, and that the stories we tell about ourselves are always shaped by what we need them to mean.
Culture, Society & the Individual
★★ Moderately probable
Past paper prompts — this theme
"Writers often use a character alienated from their culture to explore cultural or social values." (Specimen 2013)
"With reference to two works, how are social conflict and its impact portrayed?" (Nov 2024)
"Works of literature may be set in times past but their central concerns are always relevant to the time in which they were written." (Nov 2019)
The White Tiger
Adiga anatomises post-liberalisation India — a society claiming modernity while preserving feudal social structures. The "India Shining" narrative of the 2000s is satirised: Balram's entrepreneurial success is the novel's punchline, achieved through murder, ironising the discourse of meritocracy. The elections section is devastating: voters are described as "eunuchs discussing the Kama Sutra" — politically impotent, denied real participation. The corruption of every institution (schools, police, hospitals, elections) is presented as systemic, not incidental. Culture here is what prevents change; it is the operating system of class.
Satire Political critique Systemic corruption Globalisation
Key Quotes
"These are the three main diseases of this country: typhoid, cholera, and election fever... Like eunuchs discussing the Kama Sutra, the voters discuss the elections in Laxmangarh."
→ Democratic participation as illusion; political powerlessness of the poor as cultural norm
"To hell with the Naxals and their guns shipped from China. If you taught every poor boy how to paint, that would be the end of the rich in India."
→ Education and art as more radical than violent revolution; aesthetic consciousness as political threat
Fun Home
Bechdel situates her family's dynamics in 1970s–80s small-town Pennsylvania — a conservative cultural environment where Bruce's homosexuality is not explicitly condemned but simply assumed to be unspeakable. The broader culture's homophobia operates through its absence from discourse, not its presence. Bechdel contextualises her coming-out within the emergence of second-wave feminism and early LGBTQ+ visibility — Alison's generation is the first for whom an out life is imaginable. Culture in FH is the difference between one generation's death and the next generation's life.
Historical specificity Cultural artefacts in panels Silence as cultural norm Generational contrast
Key Quotes
"Grief takes many forms, including the absence of grief."
→ Cultural suppression of emotion; the Bechdel family's failure to grieve as cultural symptom
"It was a vicious cycle, though. The more gratification we found in our own geniuses, the more isolated we grew."
→ Intellectual culture as defence against emotional reality; the family's shared alienation
Sample comparative thesis
Adiga and Bechdel each depict a society in the process of defining what constitutes a legitimate subject: in WT through economic eligibility, in FH through sexual conformity — suggesting in both cases that cultural norms function not through explicit prohibition but through shaping what individuals believe to be imaginable for themselves.
Time, Memory & the Past
★★ Moderately probable
Past paper prompts — this theme
"Discuss how two works connect the past with the present." (Nov 2024)
"Some would say that we can never escape the past. Explore this idea."
"How do authorial choices regarding setting (time and place) create meaning?" (Nov 2014)
The White Tiger
The epistolary frame makes memory central to WT's form: Balram writes from a position of achieved success, constructing the past to justify the present. His account is selective, self-promotional, and retrospective — the "Darkness" of his village is described with deliberate vagueness, as though psychologically sealed off. Adiga uses this to argue that the past is always narrativised; there is no neutral account. The "seven nights" structure creates urgency but also implies that this story is time-limited — a performance before the letter closes, not a confession.
Retrospective narration Unreliable memory Selective amnesia Epistolary urgency
Key Quotes
"If only a man could spit his past out so easily."
→ The impossibility of clean rupture from origin; the past as adhesive residue
"No boy remembers his schooling like the one who was taken out of school."
→ Deprivation as the sharpest form of memory; the absent education more present than the received one
Fun Home
Fun Home is the most formally preoccupied with memory of the two works. Bechdel explicitly interrogates her own recollections — captions and images frequently diverge, enacting the instability of memory as a formal principle. She is writing into absence: Bruce died before a full reckoning could occur, so the memoir is both reconstruction and investigation. The OCD section (Chapter 5) is crucial: Alison's childhood compulsion to mark uncertainty in her diary with a symbol reveals her early awareness that subjective experience cannot be objectively transcribed — memory is always already partial.
Caption-image disjunction Non-linear chronology Writing into absence OCD as epistemological metaphor
Key Quotes
"Perhaps I'm being histrionic, trying to displace my actual grief with this imaginary trauma."
→ Self-interrogation of memory's reliability; grief's instability as a form of knowing
"You would also think that a childhood spent in such close proximity to the workaday incidentals of death would be good preparation... But in fact, all the years only made my own father's death more incomprehensible."
→ Familiarity does not produce understanding; proximity to death does not prepare one for loss
Sample comparative thesis
Both Adiga and Bechdel frame memory as an act of construction rather than retrieval — where Balram's retrospective narration in WT is explicitly self-serving and promotional, Bechdel in FH turns the same unreliability into an epistemological question, asking whether the past can be known at all when the person who shaped it is gone and the self doing the remembering is irrevocably changed.
Death, Mortality & Loss
★ Less probable
Past paper prompts — this theme
"Literature often deals with themes of coincidence, chance, or accident."
"In the past it was believed that literature showed people the way to goodness and virtue. To what extent can this be said?" (Nov 2014)
The White Tiger
Death in WT is explicitly classed. The deaths of the poor — road accidents, disease, poverty — are invisible and consequenceless; the death of Ashok generates investigation and fear of reprisal. Balram's act of murder makes him the subject rather than the object of life-and-death power for the first time. The likely deaths of his family following the murder are presented by Balram with chilling matter-of-factness — he has weighed their lives against his own freedom and chosen. This is Adiga's most uncomfortable moral demand on the reader.
Classed mortality Moral ambiguity Noir register
Key Quotes
"You can't expect a man in a dung heap to smell sweet."
→ Environment as moral determinant; refusing individual moral judgement without structural context
Fun Home
Bruce's death is simultaneously the memoir's originating event and its irresolvable mystery. Bechdel is certain it was suicide but cannot prove it — this ambiguity is structurally generative: the open question becomes the formal principle of a book that cannot close. The "fun home" (funeral parlour) literalises the cohabitation of life and death throughout her childhood. Proximity to death (embalming, burial vaults, salesmen) failed to prepare her for loss — the memoir asks what any amount of familiarity with death can actually teach us about grief.
Irresolution as structure Death and queerness intertwined Fun home symbolism
Key Quotes
"My father's death was a queer business — queer in every sense of that multivalent word."
→ Death and sexuality as inseparable; 'queer' as both identity and strangeness — Bechdel's most compressed formulation
"Grief takes many forms, including the absence of grief."
→ Emotional numbness as grief response; the Bechdel family's suppression of feeling
"Then there were those famous wings. Was Daedalus really stricken with grief when Icarus fell into the sea? Or just disappointed by the design failure?"
→ Questions whether Bruce mourned Alison's coming-out or merely saw it as a failure of his project; grief as ambiguous
Sample comparative thesis
Where Adiga deploys death as a class instrument — its visibility entirely dependent on the economic status of those who die — Bechdel leaves it irresolvably open, structuring a memoir around the impossibility of knowing how and why her father died; together they suggest that how a culture treats death reveals precisely where it locates value.
Conflict, Struggle & Juxtaposition
★★ Moderately probable
Past paper prompts — this theme
"The third question is often about some kind of conflict — between 'new' and 'old', 'rural' and 'urban', 'men' and 'women'." (Philpot guidance)
"With reference to two works, how are social conflict and its impact portrayed?" (Nov 2024)
"Fact versus fantasy: this is a clash that can have comic or tragic results."

The White Tiger
WT is structured around a series of fundamental juxtapositions: Darkness vs. Light (rural Bihar vs. urban Delhi/Bangalore), Big Bellies vs. Small Bellies, master vs. servant, old India vs. new. These are not merely thematic contrasts but spatial and economic realities that determine life outcomes. The novel's central conflict — Balram vs. the class system — is also an internal conflict: between the Balram who was conditioned by the rooster coop and the Balram who wants to break it. The murder is the resolution of this internal conflict, not merely an external act.
Binary oppositions Spatial symbolism Internal vs. external conflict Rural/urban divide
Key Quotes
"Apparently, sir, you Chinese are far ahead of us in every respect, except that you don't have entrepreneurs. And our nation, though it has no drinking water, electricity, sewage system, public transportation, sense of hygiene, discipline, courtesy, or punctuality, does have entrepreneurs."
→ Satirical juxtaposition of infrastructural failure and capitalist aspiration; India's fundamental conflict between image and reality
Fun Home
FH is built around a series of profound oppositions: surface vs. depth (the beautiful house / the unhappy family), public identity vs. private reality (Bruce the respected teacher / Bruce the closeted gay man), father vs. daughter (suppression vs. expression), past vs. present (the childhood being narrated / the adult doing the narrating). The central conflict — between the self Bruce performed and the self he actually was — is resolved only by death. Alison's conflict is between loyalty to her father's memory and the necessity of truthful self-examination.

Sample comparative thesis
Both Adiga and Bechdel structure their works around fundamental oppositions — Darkness/Light and rural/urban in WT, surface/depth and public/private in FH — but where Adiga externalises conflict as a visible economic war between classes, Bechdel internalises it as a psychological split within a single household, suggesting that the most devastating conflicts are those that are never openly declared.

Exam tips
01
Never alternate. Block structure (all of WT, then all of FH) will cost you Criterion B marks. Every paragraph must move between both texts.
02
Lead with the question, not the text. Your first sentence must address the prompt's keyword directly. "In The White Tiger, Adiga..." is a weak opening. "Both works locate [X] in the failure of [Y]..." is strong.
03
Name the technique alongside the effect. "Adiga's epistolary form creates an unreliable narrator whose self-justification..." beats "Balram tells his story in letters to..."
04
Morph the question to fit your preparation. If asked about "imprisonment," reframe as freedom / the rooster coop / Bruce's repression. Almost every topic can be redirected to your strongest material.
05
The thesis must argue, not describe. "Both works deal with identity" is not a thesis. "Both works argue that identity is assigned before it is lived, but differ in whether it can be violently reclaimed" is.
06
WT + FH strongest pairings: Identity (caste vs. sexuality), Power (class vs. domestic), Freedom (economic vs. cultural), Form (epistolary unreliability vs. graphic memoir instability), Family (hostage vs. performance).
07
Context: one sentence only. "Post-liberalisation India" or "1970s conservative Pennsylvania" — that is enough. More is plot summary.
08
Quote selection. Short, precise quotes are better than long ones. You can analyse more with "the story of a poor man's life is written on his body, in a sharp pen" than with a five-line passage.
09
Counterargument in body paragraph 3. Introduce a complication or tension in your final body paragraph ("however, this reading is complicated by...") then resolve it. This shows critical sophistication.
10
Time plan (1h45m total): 10 min planning + thesis → 30 min body para 1 → 25 min body para 2 → 25 min body para 3 → 15 min intro + conclusion + review.
